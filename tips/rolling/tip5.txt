I reviewed the uploaded VEOX data-room archive, including the current 12-slide pitch deck, executive memo, FAQ, product/architecture/security documents, benchmark/diligence materials, cap-table/use-of-funds notes, team materials, and the agent-first proof assets. I also checked VEOX’s current public positioning and current market evidence around AI adoption, AutoML, enterprise AI spending, fraud economics, and industrial downtime.

## Executive diagnosis

The current deck is **honest, disciplined, and visually clean**, but it is not yet the deck that will do VEOX justice for a wide investor audience. It reads more like a **strategic diligence memo** than a **fundraising narrative**. That is valuable in the data room, but the first-pass investor deck needs to make the opportunity feel inevitable, visible, and investable within the first 4–5 slides.

The best version of this deck should communicate one idea:

> **VEOX is building a governed algorithm-invention factory: customer data, objectives, and constraints go in; verified source-code artifacts, scorecards, and deployment packages come out.**

That is a strong story. The current deck says it, but it does not yet **show** it. There are almost no screenshots, no artifact examples, no benchmark visuals, no buyer economics, no quantified market pull, and no decisive fundraising ask. The company’s internal materials are actually stronger than the deck: the archive contains a credible claim-discipline framework, product architecture, benchmark seeds, deployment posture, IP strategy, and team story. The deck should surface those strengths more concretely.

The broad investor deck should be rebuilt around four pillars:

1. **Market pull:** AI spending and adoption are exploding, but production value remains inconsistent. Gartner forecasts worldwide AI spending near **$1.5T in 2025** and above **$2T in 2026**, while McKinsey reports AI use is widespread but only about one-third of organizations are scaling AI across the enterprise. BCG separately reports that only **5%** of companies in its 2025 survey achieved AI value at scale. ([Gartner][1])
2. **Sharp category distinction:** AutoML automates feature engineering, model selection, and hyperparameter tuning; VEOX must position itself as going one level deeper: **inventing, verifying, and shipping new governed algorithmic artifacts**, not merely selecting from known model families. ([Google for Developers][2])
3. **Concrete product proof:** The deck needs to show the artifact: source package, manifest, scorecard, deployment surface, runtime architecture, and evidence gates.
4. **Commercial wedge:** VEOX should start with high-value enterprise data problems where small measured lift can justify a large contract: fraud/risk, industrial uptime/yield, signal/mission detection, churn, routing, and portfolio-style enterprise ML optimization. Fraud-cost multipliers and downtime economics are credible investor hooks, but they should be used as buyer-urgency evidence, not as claims that VEOX has captured those dollars yet. ([PR Newswire][3])

One major warning: VEOX’s public website currently contains far more aggressive language than the data-room claim discipline allows, including language about “99% win-rates,” “most powerful,” and sweeping algorithm-invention claims. The investor deck should follow the more sober data-room standard, not the website’s most promotional wording. The public technical brief also contains a useful Engineered Intelligence definition and representative benchmark claims, but those claims should be shown only if the benchmark binder is reproducible and caveated. ([VEOX][4])

# Research-backed market framing to add

Use this in slides 2–4.

## Market context

AI is no longer early in budget terms. Gartner forecasts worldwide AI spending of nearly **$1.5 trillion in 2025** and more than **$2 trillion in 2026**. Its forecast also breaks out AI application software and infrastructure software as large, fast-growing categories. ([Gartner][1])

But adoption does not equal scaled value. McKinsey’s 2025 State of AI report says nearly nine in ten surveyed organizations use AI in at least one function, but only about one-third are scaling AI across the enterprise, and only 39% report EBIT impact. ([McKinsey & Company][5])

BCG’s 2025 AI value-gap research is even sharper: only **5%** of surveyed companies achieved AI value at scale, while 60% reported no material value. 

That is VEOX’s opening: **AI spend is huge, but the hard enterprise work is still problem-specific invention, validation, and deployment.**

## Buyer-economics framing

Do not overclaim that VEOX has already captured these outcomes. Use them to explain why buyers care.

For fraud/risk: LexisNexis reported that U.S. financial-services firms incur a total cost of **$5.75 for every $1 of fraud loss**, and U.S. lending firms incur **$5.38 per $1 of fraud loss**. ([PR Newswire][3])

For industrial uptime: ABB reported that 83% of surveyed industrial decision makers said downtime costs at least **$10,000 per hour**, and 76% estimated downtime can cost up to **$500,000 per hour**. Siemens has also framed unplanned downtime as a massive Fortune 500 cost category. ([ABB Group][6])

Use these numbers to support the statement:

> “VEOX does not need to replace the enterprise AI stack to be valuable. It needs to produce measurable lift on a small number of high-value data problems.”

## Category distinction

AutoML should be positioned respectfully, not dismissed. Google describes AutoML as automating feature engineering, algorithm selection, and hyperparameter tuning; AWS SageMaker Autopilot similarly emphasizes automatically building, selecting, and tuning models. ([Google for Developers][2])

The distinction should be:

> **AutoML asks: which known model and parameters should we use?**
> **VEOX asks: what new governed algorithmic artifact should exist for this objective, data, and deployment constraint?**

That is the clearest category boundary.

| Data-science team | custom method         | slow, scarce, non-repeatable          | repeatable governed invention  |
| Consulting/SI     | project delivery      | services-heavy, low compounding       | artifact + reusable factory    |
| Model lab/API     | general capability    | not customer-specific algorithm asset | objective-specific artifact    |
| MLOps platform    | deployment/governance | assumes model exists                  | creates and verifies method    |

This slide should be crisp and respectful. Do not over-attack established platforms.

# Claim discipline: what to say and not say

## Safe to say, based on the data room

* VEOX is building a governed algorithm-invention workflow.
* The customer-facing output is intended to be a deployable algorithmic artifact with source/package, manifest, scorecard, and deployment path.
* The current technical evidence supports a Docker-first, single-tenant runtime posture.
* Warp / Enclave / NHT are documented product surfaces.
* SageMaker compatibility can be described as a container/interface compatibility path, not full AWS automation.
* The company has a clear claim matrix and knows what is not yet proven.
* The team has relevant AI, ML systems, enterprise software, and agent-native engineering experience.

## Say carefully

* `$300k target base enterprise footprint`
  Use “target” unless signed customers exist.

* “Customers buy the artifact; VEOX keeps the factory”
  Strong, but keep “contract-defined rights” visible.

* “Benchmark outperformance”
  Use only for specific datasets, baselines, and protocols. Never generalize.

* “Agent-first engineering”
  Good as a team/building advantage, not the commercial product.

* “Security readiness”
  Say “readiness ingredients,” not compliance.

## Do not say without new evidence

* SOC 2 compliant
* production Kubernetes ready
* hosted multi-tenant cloud live
* absolute secrecy against a privileged local host
* signed revenue
* logo rights
* strategic buyer interest
* universal superiority
* 99% win rate
* “most powerful real-time algorithm inventor”
* “replaces every line of human-written code”
* RedlineDB can replace SQLite
* trading system generates production returns
* cap table fully reconciled unless counsel confirms

The current public VEOX site uses more sweeping language than the data-room deck. For investor trust, the deck should adopt the internal claim discipline and avoid the site’s more absolute phrasing. ([VEOX][4])

# How to make the deck appeal to both VCs and strategic investors

The current deck tries to serve both audiences at once. That is why the ask feels ambiguous. Create two versions.

## VC version

Tone: category creation, market timing, wedge, repeatable GTM, proof milestones.

Must answer:

* Why now?
* Why this category?
* Why this team?
* Why can this become a venture-scale platform?
* What proof exists?
* What proof will the round buy?

## Strategic/acquirer version

Tone: asset value, integration, IP, team, deployment fit, defensibility.

Must answer:

* What asset would the buyer acquire?
* What product/tech is evidenced?
* What team capability transfers?
* What integration path exists?
* What claims are not yet proven?
* What diligence risks remain?

Do not blend these in the same 12-slide main deck.
